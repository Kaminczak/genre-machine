Words for What Changed

Feed in one story · Turn the dial · Print a new world

Read the same tale twice — once as itself, once through a genre — and you will notice things changing. You already saw it. These are just the words for what you saw, sorted into the four places a change can happen.

First, two words that hold still — the frame

Every story shows up wearing a costume, and the costume tells you before you read a word what kind of trouble to expect and how you are meant to feel about it. That whole set of promises is the story's genre.

Three pigs, three houses, one wolf, one chimney. Those events happen in that order no matter which lens you put on them. What happens, in order, is the plot — and in this machine the plot is the one thing that never moves.

1 · Setting & world building

Whenever the story changes, everything you can see changes with it: how the characters are dressed, the way the trees are drawn, whether the sky is warm gold or the color of wet concrete. All of that visual surface, taken together, is the aesthetic.

Some images keep coming back until they stop being just images and start meaning something — the three houses turn into effort, or class, or safety that was never real. A picture that repeats until it carries meaning is a motif.

2 · Tone & atmosphere

Two versions can describe the exact same wolf and feel nothing alike — one nudges you to laugh at him, the other makes you check the locks. That attitude a story takes toward its own events is its tone.

Genre also decides what counts as a threat and how bad losing is allowed to be. A scraped knee in slapstick; a soul in cosmic horror; a career in a courtroom drama. What stands to be lost is the stakes.

The voiceover in noir. Once upon a time. The stamped header on a case file. Readers come expecting these and the genre hands them over. Each expected feature is a convention.

3 · Character traits

Ask what a character actually wants and why they keep going. In the fable the hare runs for bragging rights; drop him into a survival story and he is running to stay alive. What drives a character is their motivation.

Ask, too, where the teller is standing. Floating above everyone and knowing everything, locked inside one panicking character, or speaking as I from the middle of it. That vantage point is the perspective.

Every genre keeps a stock of familiar figures it reaches for: the hungry predator, the faceless government, the ancient family curse. When a story pulls one off the shelf, you are looking at a trope.

4 · Language & syntax

Somebody is always doing the telling, and you can hear them: tired and clipped in noir, grand and formal in a myth, chatty in a comedy. How that teller sounds is the story's voice.

Noir calls her a dame. A courtroom transcript calls her the witness. A myth calls her the maiden. Same woman every time — only the words moved. Word choice, and how formal it sounds, is diction.

Some versions come at you in short, hard sentences. Others wind for half a page before they let you go. How sentences get built — their length, their rhythm, the order things arrive in — is syntax.

The plot never changes. Three pigs, three houses, one wolf, every single time. When the content is already familiar, your attention is freed to watch the craft — so you stop asking what happens and start seeing how a genre makes meaning. Everything that survives the trip is the story. Everything that changed is the genre.

Part 1

Operator's Vocabulary

The dials the machine actually turns
Genre — a family of stories that share conventions a reader learns to recognize, and a writer learns to use.
Convention — an expected feature of a genre: the noir voiceover, the epic invocation, the case file's stamped headers.
Plot — what happens: the story's events, in order. The machine holds this still.
Tone — the attitude a story takes toward its own events: playful, grim, clinical, reverent.
Voice — who is telling the story, and how they sound doing it.
Perspective — the vantage point the story is told from: omniscient, third‑limited, first person.
Diction — word choice, and the formality it signals. Dame and madam name the same person.
Syntax — how sentences are built: their length, rhythm, and order. Noir clips. Gothic winds.
Aesthetic — the look and feel of a work; its whole visual and emotional style.
Motif — an image or object that recurs until it carries meaning — the three houses.
Trope — a familiar figure or device a genre reuses — the predator, the regime, the curse.
Stakes — what stands to be lost, and how badly. Genre decides what counts as a threat.
Synthesized — combined from separate parts into one new whole. The machine's final step.

# Cinderella - Bangsian

**Logline:** At the afterlife's eternal ball, Cinderella waltzes with every prince history produced, while her godmother argues precedent with the Fates: midnight, as a concept, is void here.

## Script

("She arrived in the afterlife at ninety-nine, and the first thing eternity said to her was an apology about the shoes. The Eternal Ball had been running since before her arrival — since before anyone's arrival; the dead are vague on whether it started at all or is simply the shape the evening takes here — and her invitation had, the ushers admitted, been circulating unclaimed for eighty years, since the night she lost the slipper and the story ossified around her. The Ball is held in a palace assembled from every ballroom anyone ever missed: her own palace is in there, somewhere between Versailles and a VFW hall in Ohio that a shy man never danced in. All the princes are there — history's full stock of them, hers included, dead these sixty years and holding, the ushers warned her, a table. But the afterlife's protocol dispute was already famous before she cleared the gates, and it was this: midnight. The Fates' clerk maintained that midnight, as a deadline, was a mortal instrument — time-bound, flesh-bound — and therefore void in perpetuity; nothing here ends, nothing transforms back, the gown holds, the coach idles eternally, no one need ever run. Her godmother — oh yes, godmothers die too, or at least retire here; hers had been waiting with a docket — argued the opposite as a matter of craft: the midnight was the gift, she told the tribunal. Anyone can give a girl a dress. I gave her a deadline. Remove it and you remove her, and I did not spend a career on that girl to have eternity flatten her into an ornament. The tribunal — three Fates, bored, immortal — found for the godmother in the afterlife's most-cited ruling: midnight stands, one night in every hundred, by consent of the wearer. And so it goes, the dead say. Ninety-nine nights of the century she dances unhurried among every prince and nobody's clock. And on the hundredth night the old terms resume: the gown borrowed, the hour mortal, the whole sweet machinery of almost — and at the twelfth stroke she runs, ninety-nine years old and laughing, losing one glass slipper on stairs that assemble themselves out of every stair anyone ever fled down, and her prince picks it up, as he has picked it up each century since he died, and begins looking for her all over again. Eternity watches. Eternity has season tickets. The Fates' clerk grumbles that it makes a mockery of the docket. The godmother, at her permanent table, orders another sherry and says what she always says: that is not mockery, dear. That is craft. A story is not the girl in the dress. A story is the hour she has to leave by.",)

# Cinderella - Magical Realism

**Logline:** Ash from Cinderella's hearth drifts up the chimney every dusk and settles back down as silver. The stepmother sells it. The pumpkin patch keeps a carriage on retainer. No one asks.

## Script

("The ash in that house fell upward. It had done so since the week the girl's mother died — each evening the hearth's gray lifted off the stones like weather reversing, drifted up the chimney, and came down over the roof at dusk in a fine silver settling that the neighbors called, without fuss, the mother's blessing, the way you call a draft a draft. The stepmother sold it. That was considered, by the village, her one genuinely unforgivable act — not the girl at the hearth, households arrange themselves as they will — but the ash: she had the roof swept nightly and the silver dust jarred and sold to the capital as cosmetic powder, and the village noted that the money never prospered her, arriving as it did in coins that were always, somehow, exactly cold. The pumpkin patch behind the house kept a carriage on retainer. This is simply how it was put. The vines had, for years, grown in wheel-and-axle shapes, culminating each October in one great gourd the size of a garden shed that the girl would hollow and sit in some evenings, and no one remarked, because in that village the remarkable had long ago been foldered into the chores. So when the invitation came, and the stepmother locked the girl in the kitchen out of ordinary spite, what happened next happened without astonishment: the ash came down the chimney instead of up, all of it, years of it, the sold jars emptying in the capital that same hour to their merchants' lasting confusion, and it assembled itself in the kitchen into a gown — gray, then, as she put it on, gold, the way banked embers are gray until breathed upon. The slippers arrived last, condensing out of the finest ash like dew becoming glass, still faintly warm, and they fit the way things fit when they are made of everything a mother did not get to give. The pumpkin was at the door, harnessed to itself. The village watched her ride past and waved. At the ball she danced with the prince, who was from the capital and therefore astonished by everything — the gown, the warmth of her hands, the way she checked the clock not with fear but with courtesy, like a guest minding a hostess's hours. At midnight the gown went back to ash out of politeness, having been raised properly, and the slipper she left on the stair stayed glass, because gifts, once given, do not unmake. He came to the village with it inside the week. He did not need the fitting; the shoe hummed at the kitchen door like a struck bell, and the ash on the roof came down all at once, silver, settling on his shoulders like approval. They married in October, in the pumpkin year's best carriage. The hearth in their house needs sweeping only rarely. When their first daughter cries, the ash stirs, and no one remarks, and that — the not-remarking — is the blessing, the village will tell you, that outlives every mother. Love that has become ordinary. Love that has become the house.",)

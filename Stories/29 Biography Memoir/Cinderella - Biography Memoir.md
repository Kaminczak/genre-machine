# Cinderella - Biography Memoir

**Logline:** The former queen, now widowed, writes plainly about the stepmother, the kitchen, the ball, and how little the slipper actually had to do with any of it.

## Script

("My granddaughters know the story with the pumpkin, so let me set down, while my hand is steady, the one that happened. I was in service to my stepmother — we called it family; the courts would call it wages owed, and did, eventually, but that is the end of the story and I was raised to start at the beginning. The beginning is that my mother died young and left me two things: her disposition, which is armor, and her shoes, which were glass. Not magic. Glass. My father brought them from the exposition at the capital the year they married — novelty ware, a glassblower's showpiece, dance in them once and display them after. She danced in them more than once. She was that kind of woman. The ball, then. I was twenty and not invited, and I went anyway — that detail the tellings keep, though they dress it in magic. It was not magic. It was my mother's gown taken in at the waist, my mother's shoes, and my mother's disposition. The prince — he was not a prince, he was the shipping magnate's son, but the tellings promote him — danced with me because I was the only woman in the room not performing an audition, having already concluded the evening could cost me nothing I had not already lost. Midnight is true. I left at midnight because the last tram left at midnight-ten. The shoe is true; the strap gave on the stair and I did not stop for it, trams being trams. And the search is true, though it was not romantic: he put a notice in the paper. LADY WHO LOST A GLASS SHOE AT THE HARBOR BALL MAY CLAIM IT AT THE OFFICE OF, and so on. I went for the shoe. I want that written plainly, because my granddaughters sigh at this part: I went for my mother's shoe. That I came away, in the fullness of things, with him as well — that was the second thing my mother's shoes danced home. We were married forty-one years. The shoes are in the cabinet by the stair. They have been danced in, all told, by three generations of women with one disposition. They are not magic. I have watched what they do to this family's daughters when the music starts, and I hold to my statement. They are not magic. They are permission.",)
